Sheldrick Number Calculator 202610201723

The Sheldrick number (GMSN) shows how many co-authorship steps separate someone from George M. Sheldrick (1942–2025). It is analogous to the Erdős number, but for crystallographers.

GMSN = 0 Sheldrick himselfGMSN = 1 published with himGMSN = 2 published with a co-author of hisGMSN = 3+ and so on

How the calculator works

What the results mean

Sheldrick number (GMSN)
The smallest number of co-authorship steps from the person to George M. Sheldrick. GMSN = 0 is Sheldrick himself, GMSN = 1 is anyone who published with him, GMSN = 2 is anyone who published with one of his co-authors, and so on. This is the main result. Every other figure only compares people with the same GMSN.
Newman distance
How close the person is when collaborations are weighted by strength, following Newman (2001). Each joint work with n authors adds 1/(n−1) to the tie strength of every pair of its authors. For a work on Sheldrick's list, n is the author count in the curated bibliography. For any other work, n is the number of distinct people on the OpenAlex author list, counted after the same identity merging the search uses (records of one co-author on the list, or records you ticked, count once; Sheldrick counts too). So the quality of OpenAlex's author identities affects not only whether a link exists but also its strength: a person split across two records makes n one larger, and two people wrongly merged make it one smaller. A link's length is 1 ÷ its strength, and a chain's Newman distance is the sum of its link lengths. The page reports the lowest value among all chains with the fewest steps. Lower means closer.
Vintage
The first year a chain of this length to Sheldrick existed. For each link it takes the year of that pair's first joint work. A chain only exists once all its links do, so the chain dates from its latest link. The person's vintage is the earliest such year across all shortest chains. A pair's first year is known only when all its joint works have a publication year. If some do not, the earliest dated one is only the latest possible first year, because an undated work may be earlier; a pair with no dated joint work has no known first year at all. When this leaves the vintage uncertain, the page shows the range it must lie in (for example 1985–1992) or a bound (≤ 1992). For a direct co-author it is the year of their first joint publication with Sheldrick.
Chains of people
How many different sequences of people of the minimum length lead to Sheldrick. Each chain counts once, however many papers support its links. This is the better measure of robustness, but the chains are not independent routes: two different chains can share people and links (they may differ in a single person), so removing one person or one link can cut many chains at once. Shown for GMSN 2 and above; a direct co-author has exactly one chain (straight to Sheldrick), so the page shows their joint publications instead.
Paper routes
The same chains counted paper by paper: each route picks one joint work for every link, so a link with 3 joint works triples the count. For a 2-step chain through a colleague with whom the person wrote 2 papers, and who wrote 3 with Sheldrick, it is 2 × 3 = 6. Routes through different people are added up. The number grows fast when a chain passes through one of Sheldrick's most frequent co-authors, so compare people by chains of people first.

The search, step by step

  1. Find the person. The name, ORCID or OpenAlex ID is looked up in OpenAlex. One person can be split across several OpenAlex records, so tick all of them: the ticked records are treated as one person, so their joint papers with each co-author are added up into one link and a chain through them is counted once. Records that belong to two different co-authors on Sheldrick's list are two people by the list's own account, so such a selection is refused. People further along a chain are taken as OpenAlex holds them, except for Sheldrick's co-authors, whose records are already merged in the list. Someone split across several OpenAlex records counts as several people. That can only make the GMSN and the Newman distance too high, never too low.
  2. Sheldrick's side comes from a list, not the web. His co-authors (GMSN = 1) come from the list built into this page. That makes the Sheldrick end of every chain fast and based on a curated, cross-checked reference set. Where a co-author is held by a verified ORCID, OpenAlex is asked once which author records carry it, so that co-author is recognised inside other people's papers even when the paper's author list shows no ORCID.
  3. Search outward from the person. The page fetches all of the person's works from OpenAlex and records every co-author as a person at distance 1 from them. It then fetches those co-authors' works (distance 2), and so on. This is a breadth-first search: each step is finished before the next begins. Up to 50 people are queried in each request, with up to 100 works returned per page, and up to three requests in flight at a time. Where a co-author on Sheldrick's list is known by an ORCID, OpenAlex is first asked which author records carry it, so records the same person is split across are searched too. Someone with more than 10,000 works is read year range by year range. For every query, the number of works received is checked against the number OpenAlex reports; if any are missing, the result says the search was incomplete and shows the GMSN as an upper bound (“GMSN ≤ 3”): missing works can only add links, so they can only shorten the shortest chain. The other figures get no such bound. A missing link may create a shorter chain whose Newman distance, vintage and number of chains have nothing to do with those of the longer chains found, so for an incomplete search they are shown as provisional values, computed from the works that could be read.
  4. Stop when the two sides meet. The search stops as soon as someone it reaches is on Sheldrick's co-author list. Sheldrick himself is never reached through OpenAlex: every link to him comes from the list, so a record the curated bibliography excluded cannot create one. Steps are searched in order, so the first meeting is guaranteed to be a shortest chain. The current step is always finished, so every shortest chain in the network searched is found, and the Newman distance and chain counts are exact for that network. So is the vintage when every joint work on the shortest chains has a publication year; otherwise the page gives the range it must lie in (see Vintage). The network searched is the co-authorship graph made of the works that were successfully retrieved from OpenAlex, with every link to Sheldrick taken from the curated bibliography. It is not the complete co-authorship record: works OpenAlex does not hold, holds under the wrong person, or reported but did not return are not in it (the last are detected and the result is then marked as incomplete), and the results are exact only relative to it.
  5. Check records that share an ORCID. The search is then repeated with OpenAlex author records that carry the same ORCID treated as one person, the searched person's included. These merges are not used for the reported numbers, because OpenAlex sometimes attaches an ORCID to the wrong person's record, which would merge two people and make the numbers too low. If the merged search gives a lower GMSN, a lower Newman distance or an earlier vintage, the page says so and links the records concerned, so they can be checked by hand. A shared ORCID can come to light only after two records were already met as separate people, or after a person's works were read; the check is then repeated with those records merged from the start, until a pass finds nothing new (at most 6 passes; replies already received are reused). The check searches no deeper than the first answer, and has its own request allowance equal to the budget. It resolves only the identity questions its rules allow, not every error in OpenAlex's author records: it merges records only through an ORCID that OpenAlex shows on them, so records without an ORCID, or one person under two ORCIDs, stay apart; Sheldrick's co-authors keep the identities the curated list gives them, so the check never merges two of them, and never folds another record into one of them on the strength of an ORCID the list does not hold; it cannot split a record that OpenAlex has wrongly merged; and it looks only at records the search reached. “Would not change these numbers” therefore means: not under these rules.
  6. Rank the chains. While searching, the page records how many works every pair shares, their Newman strength and their first joint year. Among the shortest chains it then finds the lowest Newman distance and the earliest vintage, and lists chains ordered by Newman distance.

Sources

  • Sheldrick's co-authors: the project's final publication and co-author tables, Sheldrick_GMSN_input.xlsx (5 October 2026): . The tables were built from Sheldrick's bibliography (807 co-authored and 37 single-author items) with full, consistent author names checked by a librarian. Name changes are one person (Herbst / Herbst-Irmer, Meyer-Bäse / Schmidt-Baese, Fittschen / Reihs); people who share a surname and initial but are different (Peter and Pierre Schaefer, J. M. and James A. Campbell) are kept apart; book editors are not co-authors.
  • Verified co-authors: the list has people, all of whom published with Sheldrick (GMSN = 1). of them can be matched to OpenAlex or ORCID and act as GMSN = 1 nodes in the search; are unreachable (no usable OpenAlex record or ORCID), but searching their name still gives their result from the list.
  • Item keys: each joint work is shown by its key in the publications table: “J” plus a number for co-authored items and “S” for single-author ones, numbered in year order. Titles and DOIs come from that table. Sheldrick's own publications list (830 entries, SHELX website) was used to cross-check it.
  • Everyone else: OpenAlex, an open index of scholarly works, queried live from your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere except OpenAlex.
  • Counting rules: on Sheldrick's side, journal articles, book chapters and proceedings papers count (see below); crystal-structure and PDB depositions do not. Beyond Sheldrick's list, the same kinds of work count by default: OpenAlex records typed as preprints, errata, datasets, front matter (paratext), peer reviews, retractions or supplementary material are left out, and so are likely meeting abstracts. Choosing “All OpenAlex records” under Options › Definition counts all of them. OpenAlex has no record type for meeting abstracts (they are filed as articles), so they are recognised by their supplement-style pagination - congress pages such as C123 or s45 (e.g. Acta Cryst. A), abstract pages such as 123a (e.g. Biophys. J.), or a supplement issue (S1, Suppl). This is a heuristic: an occasional abstract may slip through, and an occasional full paper in a supplement issue may be left out. A work that is on Sheldrick's list is never left out.
  • One person, one node: each co-author on the list is treated as a single person, matched by any of their verified OpenAlex records or their verified ORCID. OpenAlex records suspected of merging different people are quarantined and not used.

What counts as a collaboration

The Sheldrick number is meant to say “you were in the room”: you and he did the work and signed it together. A journal publication meets that test. A joint CSD or PDB deposition often does not: it records that a structure was determined with his programs, help or instrument time, which is closer to shared facilities than to joint work. So the Sheldrick number counts publications only: journal articles, book chapters and proceedings papers. On that definition people have GMSN = 1.

Data version and reproducibility

Sheldrick's side is fixed; the rest of the world is not. The Sheldrick publication and co-author reference set is a closed historical dataset: he died in 2025, the bibliography is complete, and the list built into this page will not change except by correction. Connections beyond that reference set are resolved against OpenAlex, which is re-indexed continually, so a result is reproducible only relative to the OpenAlex data available at the time of computation. A GMSN = 1 answer therefore stands on its own; a GMSN of 2 or more is a statement about the OpenAlex of a particular day. Both downloads record the retrieval window, the roster version and its hash, so a published number can be audited later against the data it rested on.

Checking your copy. The roster is embedded in this file, so an incomplete copy would otherwise look like a smaller roster rather than a broken one. On load the page checks that the embedded block is the expected length, that it holds the number of items and people it declares, and that its SHA-256 fingerprint matches the one written when the page was built (, characters). A copy that fails any of these says so in a red banner and will not compute. (A browser that cannot compute SHA-256 for a local file skips that one check; the length and count checks still apply.) The console prints the same figures on a good copy.

The co-author list embedded in this page is version (built on from Sheldrick_GMSN_input.xlsx). Every joint item carries its key, a DOI where one exists, and its title; the item numbers shown on a chain link to the DOI. The same data, with a dated provenance header, is published with the project as gmsn_data.json.

The rebuild of 5 October 2026 took every co-author from the final tables and carried over the OpenAlex records and verified ORCIDs of the earlier cleaned roster (records quarantined there as possible conflations stay quarantined). Eighteen co-authors who were not on that roster were looked up in OpenAlex directly, mostly the authors of the 2006 International Tables chapter on widely used programs. Where the librarian confirmed that one name is one person (for example Jill Trotter, Fumitoshi Hirayama, Holger Beck), only that person's OpenAlex record is used, so a namesake's papers cannot create false links.

Co-authors the calculator cannot match

people on the list have no usable OpenAlex record or ORCID. Their GMSN is 1 all the same - the curated list says so, and that is the whole evidence a GMSN = 1 answer rests on - so searching their name offers the full result, computed from the list alone without a single OpenAlex request. What the missing identifiers do prevent is the other direction: the calculator cannot recognise them inside other people's author lists, so they cannot serve as a middle link in someone else's chain, and a name match to this list is by name only, which is why the result says so and prints the items to check.

    Compared with the Erdős number

    The Sheldrick number borrows its idea from the Erdős number, which counts co-authorship steps from the mathematician Paul Erdős (1913–1996). The term was first used in print by Casper Goffman in 1969. The two work the same way, but the fields and the data behind them are very different.

    Erdős numberSheldrick number

    Tie-breakingnot usualNewman distance, then vintage

    What is the same. Both are the number of steps in the shortest co-authorship chain: 0 for the person, 1 for co-authors, 2 for co-authors of co-authors. A joint paper creates a link however many authors it has. Both centre people had unusually many collaborators, so large parts of their fields are only a few steps away.

    What is different.

    • Team size. Mathematics papers, including Erdős's, usually have few authors. Sheldrick's papers average almost five authors, and a few are large community papers such as the 94-author CCP4 description. So Sheldrick gathered more than twice Erdős's number of co-authors from fewer papers, and one large paper can link many people who hardly worked together. That is why this calculator adds the Newman distance: it gives those links little weight, much as the Erdős Number Project's two-author-only variant does, but without discarding them.
    • Kinds of work. Crystallography produces dataset depositions (crystal structures in the CCDC, protein structures in the PDB) that name authors but are not papers. There is no Erdős-number equivalent; the Sheldrick number does not count them. Citing SHELX, his software, does not count; only co-authorship does.
    • Data and scope. Erdős numbers are normally computed within Mathematical Reviews, so they cover mathematics well and other fields poorly. The Sheldrick number uses OpenAlex, which covers all fields, but its author records are generated automatically and are less carefully checked. Please check the linked papers on a surprising chain.
    • Typical values. Among mathematicians with an Erdős number, the median is 5 (mean 4.65, Erdős Number Project, 2004 data). There are no equivalent statistics for the Sheldrick number yet.
    • Extra measures. Erdős numbers are usually reported as a single number. The Sheldrick number also reports Newman distance, vintage and the number of shortest chains (of people and of papers), so people with the same GMSN can be ranked.

    Limitations

    • Author records: OpenAlex sometimes merges two people into one record or splits one person into several. A wrong merge can create a chain that does not really exist, and a split can hide one. Records that merely share an ORCID are not merged for the reported numbers; the ORCID check shows what such merges would change. Follow the linked papers and records on a chain to check it.
    • Large-team papers count fully for the Sheldrick number, as in the Erdős number convention, but add almost nothing to Newman strength. Skipping them under Options speeds up the search but may overestimate the GMSN.
    • The Newman distance is the minimum over shortest chains. A longer chain through much stronger ties can have a lower total. When the people searched include one, the page mentions it, but the true unrestricted minimum would require searching far more of the network.
